## The wrong metric and the right one

The American foodservice industry selects frying oil primarily on price per unit — 71% of operators cite it as their primary criterion (2026 State of the Fryer survey). But price per gallon tells you what the oil costs to buy. It does not tell you what it costs to use.

**Fry cycles to 25% TPM** — the number of service periods an oil remains below the Codex Alimentarius discard threshold under standardized frying conditions — is the performance metric that determines actual operational cost, food quality, and degradation product exposure. An oil that costs 30% less per gallon but lasts one-third as long costs more per productive use.

## The data sources

**Frying trial data:** Fedepalma/Universidad de Caldas (2025) standardized frying trials. Protocol: identical commercial fryers, identical food loads (potato-based products at standardized food-to-oil ratios), 180°C operating temperature, TPM measurement after each service cycle. Endpoint: 25% TPM — the NCOMA/Codex discard threshold.

**Fatty acid composition:** USDA FoodData Central (2024) and manufacturer specifications for high oleic varieties.

**Oxidative stability:** Published Rancimat OSI values (hours at 110°C) from peer-reviewed literature and manufacturer data.

## The pattern

The correlation between PUFA content and fry life is striking. The five worst-performing oils (grapeseed, soybean, corn, cottonseed, conventional sunflower) all have PUFA above 50%. The five best-performing oils all have PUFA below 15%.

This is not coincidence — it is the direct consequence of the oxidation rate differential. Choe and Min (2007) established that linoleic acid (the dominant PUFA in most seed oils) oxidizes approximately **10–40× faster** than oleic acid (the dominant MUFA in high oleic varieties). Every additional percentage point of PUFA is a percentage point of the oil that degrades at an accelerated rate.

**The practical insight:** Two oils from the same plant — conventional sunflower (66% PUFA, 107 cycles) and high oleic sunflower (9% PUFA, 210 cycles) — differ by nearly 2× in fry life. The plant doesn't matter. The fatty acid profile does.

## Aldehydes: what the oil produces as it degrades

Fry cycles measure how long the oil lasts. But the character of what it produces during degradation also matters. Grootveld et al. (2021, *Frontiers in Nutrition*) demonstrated that PUFA-rich oils generate aldehyde concentrations at frying temperature approximately **20× higher** than WHO recommended limits. MUFA-rich oils (olive, high oleic sunflower) produced substantially fewer aldehydes under identical conditions.

The specific aldehydes of concern — trans,trans-2,4-decadienal, 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), acrolein — are absorbed into the food during frying. They are what produce off-flavors (rancid, sour, fishy notes) in degraded oil, and they are what the Grootveld group has characterized as cytotoxic and genotoxic at measured concentrations.

**The implication:** An oil that lasts longer and produces fewer harmful degradation products is not just economically superior — it produces genuinely different food.

## The smoke point myth

De Alzaa et al. (2018) demonstrated that smoke point does not predict frying performance. EVOO outperformed higher-smoke-point canola and grapeseed in both oxidative stability and polar compound formation. In extended trials, EVOO lasted over 28 hours of continuous frying versus 18–20 hours for higher-smoke-point alternatives.

The Oil Atlas reports smoke point for reference, but we do not rank by it. **OSI (Oxidative Stability Index)** and fatty acid composition are the predictive metrics.
