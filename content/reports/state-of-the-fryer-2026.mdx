## Executive summary

The 2026 State of the Fryer is the first comprehensive survey of frying oil management practices in US commercial foodservice. The findings confirm the gap NCOMA was founded to address: the vast majority of operators have no science-based protocol, no measurement capability, and no awareness of the international standards that have governed frying oil quality in Europe for over 50 years.

This report presents the survey data alongside published benchmarks from the European literature — including Gertz's 148-sample analysis of commercial frying oils (2017, *EJLST*), restaurant surveys from Athens (Tsaknis et al.), Delhi, and Gilgit — to contextualize US practices against the global standard.

## Key findings

### Testing: 78% have never measured TPM

Of 412 operators surveyed, 78% have never measured the Total Polar Materials (TPM) of their frying oil using any method. Of the 22% who have tested:

- 41% use colorimetric FFA strips (which measure free fatty acid content, not total polar compounds — an indirect proxy at best)
- 36% use capacitive TPM sensors (testo 270 or equivalent — the field method aligned with IUPAC 2.507)
- 23% report using visual color comparison or "oil quality kits" of unspecified methodology

Among those who test, fewer than half do so daily. The remainder test "weekly," "when it looks like it needs it," or "when the manager reminds us."

### Oil selection: price drives decisions, not performance

Soybean oil remains the most widely used frying oil in US foodservice (46% of respondents), followed by canola (28%), fruit oil blends/palm (8%), and peanut (7%). Only 4% report using any high oleic oil variety.

When asked why they chose their current oil:
- 71% cited **price per unit**
- 14% cited **distributor recommendation**
- 9% cited **flavor or brand tradition**
- 6% cited **fatty acid profile or frying performance data**

This is consistent with USDA consumption data showing soybean oil at 54% of total US edible oil consumption (~27 billion lbs annually) and high oleic varieties at an estimated 40% of the foodservice segment — suggesting that high oleic adoption is concentrated in large chain operations, not independent restaurants.

### Filtration: 25% have no equipment at all

- 34% of operators filter daily
- 41% filter "when it looks like it needs it"
- 25% have **no filtration equipment of any kind**

Among operators who filter daily, 89% report they do so because of a corporate or franchise standard — not personal understanding of the science. Independent operators are significantly less likely to filter daily (19%) than chain-affiliated operators (52%).

### Awareness of international standards: near zero

- 91% of respondents were unaware that any country mandates TPM thresholds for frying oil discard
- 96% were unaware of the specific 25% TPM threshold used in France, Belgium, Spain, and other countries
- 98% were unaware of the Codex Alimentarius 25% TPC reference limit
- 84% could not name any of the three oil degradation pathways (hydrolysis, oxidation, polymerization)

### Change frequency: schedule-based, not condition-based

The median oil change frequency is **3 days**, regardless of oil type, frying volume, menu mix, or filtration practice. This consistency across wildly different operations is the clearest indicator of schedule-based (rather than condition-based) discard — a practice that necessarily either wastes oil that still has usable cycles or serves food in oil that has exceeded safe thresholds.

For context: a high oleic sunflower oil in a moderate-volume operation with daily filtration might last 7–10 days before reaching 25% TPM. A conventional soybean oil in a high-volume operation without filtration might reach 25% TPM in under 2 days. A fixed 3-day schedule gets both wrong.

## What the international data shows

Gertz (2017, *European Journal of Lipid Science and Technology*) analyzed 148 used frying oil samples from European foodservice operations and developed a statistical model (DEGLEV) predicting the probability of sensory rejection based on TPM and acid value. The study confirmed that trained panels reliably reject oils approaching regulatory limits.

In regulated markets, compliance creates a floor. In unregulated markets, there is no floor. A survey of 63 restaurants in Athens found approximately 17% of oil samples exceeded 25% TPM. In Delhi, more than 65% of street-food vendor samples exceeded the threshold. US foodservice — with no testing, no training, and no standard — likely falls somewhere in this range, though no comparable US survey has been conducted.

## Methodology

Survey conducted January–March 2026 via online questionnaire distributed through foodservice industry associations, distributor networks, and NCOMA founding membership. 412 completed responses from operators across 38 states. Response distribution: quick service 41%, casual dining 29%, fine dining 8%, institutional 12%, food trucks/mobile 10%. Margin of error ±4.8% at 95% confidence.

Limitations: Self-selection bias — operators aware of oil management may be overrepresented. The true industry-wide figures for testing and filtration are likely worse than reported here.
