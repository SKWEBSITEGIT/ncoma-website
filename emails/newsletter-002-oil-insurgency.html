Flesh, not seed.

In Issue #1, we asked the question: what is in your frying oil? The answer, for most American kitchens, is a cocktail of degraded seed oils — canola, soybean, sunflower — that break down fast, absorb into food, and produce compounds linked to serious health risks.

But here’s what most operators don’t know: not all cooking oils degrade at the same rate. The chemistry of the oil — specifically its fatty acid profile — determines how long it lasts, how much it oxidizes, and what it produces when it breaks down.

This issue, we go deeper into the science — and introduce you to a NCOMA partner that’s bringing a fundamentally different oil to the US market.

The Science

Fruit oil vs. seed oil: why the source matters.

Walk into any commercial kitchen in America. Open the fryer. What’s in it? Almost always a seed oil — soybean, canola, sunflower. Extracted from seeds with chemical solvents, bleached, deodorized. Cheap to buy. Expensive to use.

Why expensive? Because seed oils break down fast. They’re loaded with polyunsaturated fats that oxidize at frying temperatures — producing the aldehydes, free radicals, and off-flavors we talked about in Issue #1. A typical soybean oil is done in 110 fry cycles. Your kitchen is changing oil constantly.

Now compare that to a fruit oil — pressed mechanically from the flesh of a fruit, the way olive oil is made. Different source, different chemistry, dramatically different performance in the fryer.

Meet The Oil Insurgency.

Most operators don’t choose their oil — their distributor chooses it for them. It’s usually the cheapest seed oil available: canola, soybean, or a blend. Anonymous. Solvent-extracted. Gone in 100–150 fry cycles.

The Oil Insurgency is building a different model. Their product — High Oleic Palm Olein (HOPO) — is mechanically pressed from the fleshy mesocarp of the Colombian OxG palm fruit. Not from a seed. Not with solvents. From a fruit, the same way olive oil is made.

In a controlled trial at 175°C, The Oil Insurgency HOPO cleared 300+ fry cycles before crossing the regulatory discard threshold. Every PUFA-heavy seed oil tested failed by cycle 150.
